Colour System

Word-Highlighting Technique

Zendesk highlights only the outcome word or contrasting punchline phrase in lime — never the subject or verb. The lime word is the first place a scanning eye lands, instantly communicating value even without reading the full sentence.

AI that gets smarter with every resolution.

→ "smarter" = the outcome. Highlighted. Subject and noun stay white.

Move beyond deflection. Deliver real resolutions.

→ Entire punchline phrase goes lime when it contrasts an emotional setup.

Designed for AI-first customer service

→ Differentiating qualifier highlighted, not the generic noun.

Feature Deep-Dive
Scrollytelling Story Cards
Off-white bg · ~800px total height · 3-card stack
  • BackgroundWhite page with large rounded cards (border-radius: 24px) on off-white/light-grey fill #F2F1EC. Cards appear to "float" on the page.
  • Card layoutEach card: two-column 50/50 split. [Left: H3 text block flush left] · [Right: product UI screenshot or chat mockup]. Cards are ~500px tall each.
  • Connecting elementA vertical timeline line runs down the centre between cards. Circular node icons (question mark → spinner → checkmark) animate as user scrolls — creating a narrative journey: problem → processing → resolution.
  • Card titles24–28px · weight 700 · near-black · left-aligned. Examples: "Every conversation becomes a learning signal", "Zendesk AI turns signals into improvement", "Faster resolutions. Stronger loyalty. Better outcomes."
  • UI mockup imagesRight side of each card: floating chat interface screenshots. Border-radius ~14px. Shadow 0 4px 16px rgba(0,0,0,.1). Show real product UI interactions. Size: ~380px wide.
  • PaddingCards: 40–48px internal. Gap between cards: ~24px. Section: 80px top/bottom.
Strategy

Scrollytelling converts passive reading into active participation. The user scrolls, the story advances. The three-act narrative (problem → AI learns → outcome delivered) maps directly to the buying concern: "Will this actually work?" The timeline icons are a clever shorthand — the UX of customer service compressed into three symbols. Product screenshots on the right anchor the emotional copy in operational reality.

Section 04
Copy Tone & Voice

How Zendesk writes. The rules behind every headline, subheading, button label, and body paragraph — and why each choice is made for conversion.

Confident, Not Arrogant

Zendesk makes bold claims but grounds every one in specifics: "80% automation", "22K+ teams", "4.8B resolutions". No vague superlatives like "best" or "leading".

"Achieve up to 80% automation with AI Agents that continuously learn."

Outcome-First Language

Every headline leads with the customer outcome, not the product feature. Not "AI Agents available" — "Deliver real resolutions." The product is always in service of the result.

"Move beyond deflection. Deliver real resolutions."

Punchy, Sentence-Fragment Style

Headlines frequently use fragments or pairs of short sentences: "Faster resolutions. Stronger loyalty. Better outcomes." — a list-within-a-headline technique that feels decisive.

"Faster resolutions. Stronger loyalty. Better outcomes."

Direct Second-Person ("you", "your")

Section 06
Strategy Rationale

The "why" behind the entire design system — the buyer psychology, conversion architecture, and brand positioning decisions that make it work.

Page Flow = Funnel Architecture
The page is structured as an explicit conversion funnel: Capture attention (hero, announcement) → Build desire (value prop, scrollytelling, stats) → Eliminate objections (analyst report, testimonials) → Enable decision (use-case cards, pricing CTA). Every section has a single job in the funnel. There's no section that's "decorative."
The Dual-CTA System Throughout
At every conversion moment, Zendesk offers two options: a high-commitment action (Try for free / Contact sales) and a lower-commitment one (View demo / Learn more). This is based on research showing that offering a "step down" option captures visitors who aren't ready to commit, without diluting the primary CTA's prominence. The primary is always lime; secondary is always outline.
Colour as Conversion Architecture
Lime is used exclusively for conversion elements (buttons, highlighted words, stat numbers, active indicators). This trains the visitor's eye: lime = action or value. After three seconds on the page, the subconscious associates lime with "click this" or "this matters." No decorative lime use anywhere — every lime element is functional.
Dark Sections as Authority Markers
Dark green sections appear at three moments: the feature showcase (we have substance), the analyst validation (external authority), and the final CTA (make your decision). The colour shift from white to dark is a psychological "weight" signal — the page becomes more serious. Visitors subconsciously give these sections more credibility.
Product Proof Without a Product Demo
Zendesk shows product UI screenshots inside every major section — but never as a full product tour. The floating chat overlay in the hero, the mockups in scrollytelling cards, the screenshots in tab panels — all are "glimpse" moments. They prove the product is real and functional without overwhelming a marketing page with interface complexity.
Trust Layer Stacking
Trust signals accumulate in a deliberate sequence: (1) Logo bar = social proof by association. (2) Animated stats = scale proof by data. (3) Gartner report = expert validation. (4) Testimonial with metrics = peer proof. By the time a visitor reaches the bottom CTA, they've been given four different categories of trust evidence. The final objection is price — which the FAQ link handles.
Content Marketing as Retention
The resources grid (Report, Guide, Article) serves visitors who are not yet ready to buy. By surfacing useful content, Zendesk captures email and intent from researchers. These visitors enter a content nurture funnel and eventually convert — often at higher deal values than visitors who clicked "Try for free" on day one.
Whitespace = Premium Positioning
Zendesk allocates approximately 40% of each section to white space. This is a deliberate premium signal: expensive brands give their content room to breathe. Dense pages feel cheap and anxious. The generous spacing also improves reading comprehension by reducing cognitive load, which means visitors retain more of the message.

LIFESTYLE PHOTO Section S3 — Hero Stage 1 — Aspiration

Image 01 — Hero Background: Woman with Lime Headphones

Subject & Composition

A woman, eyes gently closed, wearing lime-green headphones (exact colour match to #BAE53B brand accent). She sits in warm golden ambient light with a softly blurred aircraft window behind her. Psychology & Strategy

The closed-eye expression signals complete comfort and trust — a deeply aspirational state for service software buyers who typically live in customer complaint stress. The golden warmth triggers the brain's reward association. The headphones are the critical bridging device: they are the same colour as every CTA button, subliminally linking the feeling of delight to the act of clicking. This is brand colour as emotional anchor, not mere aesthetics.

What It Communicates

  • "Your customers feel this when service is effortless."
  • Premium, modern, global (travel setting = international brand).
  • Human-centred product, not a cold enterprise tool.
  • Zendesk = resolution, calm, happiness — not tickets and queues.

Desktop vs Mobile

Desktop (1440px): Full-width background image, right-side composition. Headline + form overlays the left empty space. Image visible immediately on page load, above the fold.
Mobile (390px): Intentionally hidden above the fold. Text and CTA load first, converting intent before showing imagery. The hero image appears ~1 scroll below fold — a deliberate performance + conversion trade-off. The image crops to show her face/upper body, not the full compositional scene.
Key Insight: The exact matching of headphone colour to the lime CTA is not coincidence — it is a cross-sensory conversion technique. The eye subconsciously links emotional resonance with the call-to-action colour, lowering friction on the click.

PRODUCT UI Section S6 — Scrollytelling (3 screens) Stage 2 — Proof

Image 03 — Scrollytelling Product Mockups: The Three-Act UI Story

Three sequential product UI screenshots that tell a before/during/after narrative of AI learning in action. This is the highest-density proof section on the page.

Act 1 — The Problem

Chat conversation showing a bot failing — a customer asking a complex question that the AI cannot fully resolve. This screen is deliberately shown to create relatability: every CX team knows this pain. It establishes the problem Zendesk solves.

Act 2 — The Intelligence

Knowledge gap analysis dashboard with a dark header "Fill knowledge gaps," showing flagged content topics, a "High Priority" amber badge, and a "Suggest sources" lime CTA. This screen shows the AI not just reacting but learning — a premium differentiator over rule-based bots.

Act 3 — The Resolution

Personalised chat response: "Hi Ariel. Sure thing, I'll take care of that for you." — the AI addresses the customer by name and delivers a warm, confident, human-sounding resolution. The contrast with Act 1 is the payoff: same scenario, dramatically better outcome.

Why Three Screens, Not One

A single "happy path" UI screenshot would be unconvincing — buyers are cynical. By showing the problem first, Zendesk demonstrates self-awareness and positions the product as the solution to a pain the buyer already feels. The three-screen sequence creates a micro-story that buyers mentally map onto their own situation.

Desktop vs Mobile

Desktop: Large sticky-scroll experience. Each UI screenshot occupies ~50% viewport width, pinned while copy scrolls alongside. Images are oversized for detail legibility.
Mobile: Vertical stacked timeline. Each card shows the UI mockup as a small thumbnail (≈120px wide) right-aligned within a rounded card, with the narrative text left-aligned. The visual story is preserved but compressed.

Universal Imagery Principles — Applicable to Any Industry

1. Colour Echo Principle

The hero image contains a physical object in the exact brand accent colour (lime headphones = lime CTA). This creates a subconscious loop: the emotional response to the image is neurologically pre-loaded onto the action you want the user to take. Template variable: place a physical object in your brand's primary conversion colour within the hero image.

2. Emotion Before Evidence

Lifestyle photography comes first (hero) to establish feeling, then product UI screenshots come second to provide rational proof. Never lead with product UI — it triggers analytical mode before emotional buy-in is established. On mobile, this is compressed: emotion and proof must share the same vertical space without competing.

3. Illustration for Complexity, Screenshot for Specifics

Use illustrated product mockups when you need to communicate a concept (omnichannel = orbital icons). Use actual screenshots when you need to prove a detail (keyword highlighted in chat = NLP works). The rule: illustration explains, screenshot proves. Mixing both maximises both comprehension and credibility.

4. Third-Party Images Build More Trust Than Own Images

The Gartner chart, partner logos, and customer testimonial photography all involve imagery you don't fully control — and that's the point. Every third-party image says "external voices agree with us." Design space for third-party evidence: don't resize, recolour, or modify analyst charts. Their institutional authority is the asset.

5. Mobile Image Strategy: Prioritise, Don't Shrink

Zendesk makes active decisions to remove images on mobile (landscape photography, testimonial photos) rather than just making them smaller. Mobile images are chosen for their narrative-critical role, not retained out of desktop parity. Ask: "If I can only show 1 image in this section on mobile, which one does the most work?" That's your mobile image.

6. Demographic Diversity as Trust Signal

Every human face across Zendesk's imagery is from a different demographic (age, ethnicity, gender). This is not token representation — it is a product claim: "Zendesk serves global customer bases." The customer avatar in the voice call UI, the woman in the hero, the report photography, the testimonial subjects — each reaches a different segment while collectively signalling universality.
